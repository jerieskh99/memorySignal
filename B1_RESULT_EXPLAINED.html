Understanding the B1 result — in plain terms

The first analysis-stage result (Plan 08 / B1, the encoding floor), from the ground up, no jargon. What the experiment does and what the numbers mean.

Why this exists. A plain-language companion so you — and any future chat or agent — can read the numbers (recall, APF vs wAPF, the three splits) without re-deriving them. The technical log is plan08_overview.html; the pre-registration is docs/EXPERIMENT_B1_ENCODING_FLOOR.md; the raw numbers are b1_ae_results.json.

Step 1
What are we even looking at?

Every half-second we photograph the computer's memory and compare it to the previous photo. For each 4 KB chunk (a page) we note how much it changed. Then we squash the whole photo-diff into one number: "how busy was memory this instant?"

Two flavors: APF = what fraction of pages changed (yes/no, averaged). wAPF = the same, but each page counts by how much of it changed. Do that every half-second and you get a wiggly line over time — a heartbeat monitor for memory. One line per recording.

Step 2
What is a "window"?

We chop that heartbeat line into pieces of 8 readings in a row (stepping 4, so they overlap). Each 8-number piece is one window — think of an 8-second clip of a song. One recording gives ~170 clips. Every clip is one thing we classify.

Step 3
What are we trying to do with a clip?

Sort it into its family: was this memory activity made by a memory program, a cpu program, an app, a disk (io) program, etc.? Seven families. We guess the family from the clip alone — we never see the actual program.

Step 4
How do we guess? (the "autoencoder bank")

We train one expert per family. Each studies only its own family's clips until it knows them. To classify a new clip, we ask all 7 experts "how familiar is this?" and give the clip to whichever is most comfortable.

The "expert" is a small neural net (an autoencoder) that squeezes a clip into 3 numbers and rebuilds it. It rebuilds its own family's clips well and unfamiliar ones badly, so "badly rebuilt" means "not my family."

Step 5
What is "recall"? (the number that carries the story)

Recall answers one specific question, per family:

Of all the clips that truly belong to this family, what fraction did we correctly catch?

If 100 clips are truly mem and we correctly label 75 of them "mem", then mem recall = 75/100 = 0.75. So recall = "did we find the ones that really belong here?" — a per-family batting average.

app recall 0.84 → we caught 84% of real app clips. cpu recall 0.02 → we caught almost no cpu clips; they got labeled as something else. Recall shows, family by family, which families the instrument can find and which it is blind to.

Step 7
Why?

The instrument only sees memory writes. Programs that mostly compute or read (a cpu hashing loop, threads fighting over a lock) barely write, so their clips are almost all near-zero — flat lines. And one flat line looks like every other: the experts for cpu/io/thread cannot tell them apart, so they mislabel each other and recall goes to ~0. app and mem write a lot → real shape → recognized.

This "loud vs quiet" split is the instrument's write-only blind spot — predicted years ago ("only writes are visible"), measured for the first time here. It is mutual, not random: cpu is mislabeled as thread, thread as io — the quiet families pour into one indistinguishable pool.

lowo 0.342 beats blind guessing (0.234) but not by much — 5 of 7 families are invisible and drag the average down. Crucially, at the ceiling the visible families are near-perfect (mem 1.00, app 0.98, io 0.97), so the model is not broken — it separates families fine where there is a sound to hear.

Step 9
APF vs wAPF (the actual experiment)

We ran the whole thing twice — once with APF clips, once with wAPF clips. APF won everywhere. The families we can see (memory sweeps) change many pages a tiny bit each; wAPF weights by "how much," shrinking them toward zero — into the same flat-line blind spot as the quiet families. APF just counts pages, keeping them visible. For telling families apart, the plain count beats the weighted amount. Clearest at the ceiling: mem falls from 1.00 (APF) to 0.66 (wAPF).

Step 10
The full picture in one breath

We turn memory into a busyness-heartbeat, slice it into 8-beat clips, and ask a panel of family-experts which family each clip belongs to. Recall tells us, per family, how many of that family's clips we caught. The result: the instrument catches the two families that write heavily (app, mem), is deaf to the three that mostly compute (cpu, io, thread), and the plain count (APF) beats the weighted version (wAPF). That is the floor everything else gets measured against.

The one open question that changes the verdict. wAPF is APF times an intensity — a product, and a product hides its factors. So wAPF losing is ambiguous: maybe intensity carries nothing, or maybe multiplying it into one number destroyed it. The next experiment (E2) keeps breadth and intensity as two separate channels. If E2 beats APF, the loss was the product; if E2 matches APF, intensity adds nothing for family classification. That single test turns "weighting lost" into the real verdict.
Remember this. The instrument hears writers and is deaf to thinkers; among the writers, counting beats weighting; and the honest score rests on two families, not seven. Everything the next experiments do is measured against that.

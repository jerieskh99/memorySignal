Entry 14 · September 2026 · Plan 08 (B1, the encoding floor)

The first thing the analysis saw

Everything until now was building the instrument. This is the first time it was pointed at a real question and asked to answer: given a short window of memory activity, which kind of workload made it? The answer is lopsided, honest, and exactly the shape the earlier entries predicted.

The one idea to hold onto

The instrument recognizes the workloads that write loudly and is deaf to the ones that compute quietly. And where it hears anything at all, counting the pages that changed beats weighting them by how much — because what little it hears, it hears by breadth.

1
The setup

Two numbers, a window, a bank of listeners

From the captured traces we take one number per snapshot, two ways. The Active Page Fraction (APF) is how many pages changed. The weighted APF (wAPF) is the same average but each page counts by how much of it changed — the count replaced by the amount. Both are computed from the per-page Hamming already stored in the traces; no raw memory is re-read.

Each is then cut into windows of 8 consecutive snapshots (hop 4). One window — eight numbers — is one sample. To decide a window's family we train one autoencoder per family on that family's windows, then ask every family's autoencoder to rebuild the new window; the family whose autoencoder rebuilds it best (lowest reconstruction error) wins. A bank of listeners, each tuned to one family's sound, and the window goes to whoever recognizes it.

In plain terms

Turn each trace into a thin strip of "how busy was memory" numbers, slice it into eight-at-a-time windows, and hand each window to a panel of judges — one per family. Each judge only really knows its own family. The window is assigned to whichever judge is least surprised by it.

2
The reassurance

It works — where there is a sound to hear

The first fear with any weak-looking number is that the model is broken. It is not. When the judges are tested on windows from traces they trained on (the memorization ceiling), the loud families are recognized almost perfectly:

familyceiling recall (APF)
mem1.00heavy writers
app0.98databases, compression, parsing
io0.97file writers
cpu0.11compute — barely writes
thread0.02synchronization — barely writes
What this settles

Three families are near-perfect even at the ceiling, so the machinery separates families fine when there is something to separate. The low overall score is not a broken model — it is a handful of families that carry it while the rest contribute nothing. cpu and thread are near-zero even while memorizing: the model cannot tell them apart even when it is allowed to cheat.

3
The blind spot, measured

The quiet families collapse into one

Entry 4 warned it in one sentence: the instrument records only writes. A workload that mostly computes or reads — a hashing loop, a lock fight, a cache scan — barely writes memory, so its windows are almost all zeros. And to a detector that only hears writes, one silence sounds like another.

Why "mutual" matters

If cpu, io and thread scattered at random, the model would be broken. Instead they land on each other — cpu becomes thread, thread becomes io. That is the signature of families that are genuinely identical in this representation, not of a faulty judge. The negative is real and it is a property of the instrument, not a bug.

4
The encoding comparison

Weighting made it worse, and we know why

This whole plan exists to compare two encodings: APF (count) against wAPF (weighted amount). wAPF lost on every split. That is worth understanding rather than shrugging at, and the reason is mechanical.

The families the instrument can see are the memory sweeps — and a sweep writes one byte to a great many pages. It has huge breadth and tiny intensity. APF, which counts breadth, places it at the loud end (a quarter of all pages moving). wAPF, which weights by intensity, shrinks it back toward zero — straight into the silent blob with the quiet families. The clearest reading is at the ceiling: mem recall falls from 1.00 under APF to 0.66 under wAPF.

Takeaway

Family separation here is carried by breadth — how many pages moved — which is exactly what APF measures and what weighting throws away. So for telling families apart, the plain count wins. This is a genuine negative for wAPF, and the contribution is knowing precisely why it loses.

5
The result, stated honestly

The honest headline, and what it is not

The number that counts is the hardest split: hold out an entire workload the model has never seen, and ask which family it belongs to. On that test only two families survive:

family (unseen workload)APFwAPF
app0.840.54
mem0.750.64
io / cpu / thread~0.02~0.01
cache / sandbox0.000.00
Be honest about the limits

Only the two high-write families generalize to an unseen workload. Five of seven are invisible or do not generalize. cache is three tiny singleton workloads; sandbox is a single workload held out with nothing left to train, so its zero is by construction, not by failure. This is one task (family), one model (the autoencoder bank), one window size (8/4, inherited). It is a floor, not a verdict on the whole method.

Read the way this project reads its own results, that is not a disappointment. "Half of computation is invisible to a write-only memory signal, and the visible half is separated by breadth, not intensity" is a finding. The negatives are the contribution.

6
Still open

The one test that is still open

wAPF is APF times an intensity — a product. A product hides its factors: a few pages fully rewritten and many pages barely touched can land on the same number. So wAPF's loss is ambiguous. It could mean intensity carries nothing, or it could mean that multiplying it into a single number is what destroyed it.

Proposed — not yet run

The E2 arm. Keep breadth and intensity as two separate channels rather than one product (sixteen numbers per window instead of eight). If E2 beats APF, the loss was the collapse into one number and intensity does carry signal. If E2 matches APF, intensity genuinely adds nothing for family classification. That single test turns "weighting lost" into the real verdict.

After that come the unsupervised models — do the families fall out on their own, without labels? — and, much later, the richer encodings this floor exists to be measured against. For now, the floor is set: the plain count of changed pages, on the workloads that write.

What to remember

The instrument hears writers and is deaf to thinkers; among the writers, breadth beats weighted amount; and the honest score rests on two families, not seven. Everything the next experiments do is measured against that.

Glossary

Hover any dotted term for its definition.

Active Page Fraction (APF)
The fraction of memory pages that changed between two snapshots — a count of how many pages moved, ignoring how much.
Weighted APF (wAPF)
The same average, but each page counts by how many of its bits changed instead of a flat one. The count replaced by the amount.
Window
Eight consecutive snapshots of one signal, stepped by four. One window is one sample handed to the model.
Autoencoder
A small network trained to squeeze its input into a few numbers and rebuild it. Trained on one family, it rebuilds that family's windows well and unfamiliar ones badly.
Reconstruction error
How far the autoencoder's rebuilt window is from the real one. Low means "this looks like what I was trained on." The family with the lowest error wins the window.
Ceiling
Testing on windows from traces the model trained on — the easiest possible test. A low ceiling means the families are genuinely inseparable, not that the model is under-trained.

The instrument was lying by 15×

Before choosing how fast to sample, one question had to be answered: does the pipeline actually honour the rate it claims? It did not — and had never done so. Four days, five rounds, and twelve separate defects later, it did.

The one idea to hold onto

You cannot trust a measurement made by an uncalibrated instrument. Every confusing result in the previous entry was computed against a time step that was fifteen times wrong.

1
The headline bug

A missing utility, and a sleep that never slept

The first calibration run took three snapshots over fifteen seconds with the sampling interval configured to 100 milliseconds. The measured interval — the time the guest was actually allowed to run between snapshots — was 0.0066 seconds. The virtual machine was frozen for 99.8% of the wall clock.

The cause was six lines of shell:

if command -v bc &>/dev/null; then
  sleep "$(echo "scale=3; $intervalMsec/1000" | bc)"
else
  # bash integer arithmetic: 100 / 1000 == 0
  sleep "$(( intervalMsec / 1000 ))"
fi

The calculator utility bc was not installed on the capture server. So the fallback ran, and integer division took 100 milliseconds to zero. The command was sleep 0. The interval setting had never set anything at all.

Why this is worse than a slow pipeline

The analyzer was told the spacing between frames was 0.100 seconds. It was 0.0066. Every frequency it computed was therefore placed fifteen times wrong on the spectrum. Workloads with genuinely different rhythms were being compared on a mislabelled axis — which is exactly the "confusion between workloads that should have separated easily" from the previous entry.

Analogy

Filming something at an unknown frame rate and then playing it back assuming 24 frames per second. Nothing in the footage is corrupt. Every conclusion about how fast anything moved is wrong.

After installing the missing utility, the second run measured 0.125 s ± 5 ms at a 100 ms setting — the requested interval plus about 25 ms of unavoidable shell bookkeeping, stable to within 4.2%. The knob was connected to reality for the first time.

Takeaway

The bug was not in the analysis, the metric, or the model. It was one absent command-line tool on one server, silently swallowed by a fallback written to be helpful.

2
The reframe

Three clocks, and which one the analyzer needs

The instrumentation added six timestamps around every snapshot: before the freeze, after the freeze is confirmed, either side of the memory copy, and either side of the thaw. With those in place, a confusion that had already produced one wrong conclusion could be named and separated.

The study that opens this period does so with a correction against its own earlier revision, which had "conflated host wall-clock cost per snapshot with analysis frame spacing in guest time" and concluded the effective time step was 2.5 to 5.5 seconds. Its verdict on itself: "That conclusion was incorrect."

In plain terms

Two different questions were being answered with one number. "How often does the workload get a moment to run?" and "how long does one full snapshot cost me in real time?" are not the same, and only the first belongs on a frequency axis.

A number worth keeping

Copying one gigabyte of guest memory measured 0.755 s ± 0.010, about a third of the 2.5 s the original planning document had assumed. Meanwhile confirming the freeze averaged 3.66 s with wild variance — dominated not by the copy but by a polling loop waiting for the machine to report itself paused. The expensive part was not the part anyone had budgeted for.

3
The isolation test

The consumer was strangling the producer

The pipeline has two halves: one takes snapshots, the other computes differences between them. Running the second half with the first was making the first far slower than anyone had attributed to it. Turning the consumer off and re-measuring:

MeasurementConsumer runningConsumer offChange
Confirming the freeze6.380 s0.062 s102× faster
Copying memory1.720 s0.900 s1.9× faster
Full cycle9.253 s1.642 s5.6× faster
Time guest spent frozen99.8%92.3%—

And the trade-off across sampling rates, measured rather than assumed — the fraction of real time the guest spends frozen, at each interval setting:

interval    100ms   250ms   500ms   1000ms   2000ms
frozen      92.5%   84.8%   74.2%   59.4%    42.5%
Where this earns its keep

This table is the honest price list for the whole method. Sampling four times faster does not cost four times more — it costs a steadily larger share of the guest's life, and past a point the machine barely runs at all. Every later decision about sampling rate is an argument with this table.

4
The best bug of the four days

The pause that was secretly doing something else

The producer had a half-second pause after each copy, put there to let the filesystem settle. The question was whether removing it made things faster. The experiment said yes. Then a later round said no, decisively, in the opposite direction:

Round 3 · cleanup onRound 4 · cleanup off
Effect of removing the pause−0.515 s (faster)+2.466 s (slower)
Copy time, 5th snapshot0.77 s1.95 s
Copy time, 6th snapshot0.77 s7.25 s

The only difference between the rounds was that the second had dump cleanup switched off, deliberately, so a different check would have files left to inspect. With cleanup off, dirty pages piled up, and by the sixth snapshot every memory copy was blocked waiting for the operating system to flush them to disk.

Takeaway

The half-second pause was never a filesystem courtesy. It was accidentally acting as a dirty-page barrier — and its benefit only appeared once a different setting made dirty pages accumulate. Two knobs that looked independent were coupled through the kernel.

The resolution was to measure before the drift set in. Recomputing on the first five snapshots recovered the original answer (−0.481 s), and the sign of the result turns out to depend entirely on how many snapshots you average over:

first 5 snapshots   −0.481 s   removing the pause helps
first 6 snapshots   −0.291 s
first 7 snapshots   +0.609 s   ← the answer flips sign here
first 10 snapshots  +2.466 s   drift now dominates the average

The fix was one line — change the default window from ten snapshots to five — justified directly off the point where the drift begins. Final verdict, written by the orchestrator into its own results file:

Round 5 verdict · 2026-05-21

"REMOVE the flush — dumps remain intact and host_dt drops by 0.380 s (25.0%) on the first-5 comparison."

About a tenth of the wall-clock cost of every snapshot, for the rest of the project. Twelve mechanisms and bugs were closed across the five rounds.

5
The recurring shape

Two checks that were wrong about healthy data

Two of the four experiments in this round produced garbage, and in both cases the fault was in the checking apparatus rather than the pipeline.

The first. Every result file reported zero dumps removed, and memory copies that should have taken under a second were taking 15 to 31. The deletion call was failing with a permission error — because deleting a file requires write permission on the directory, not on the file. The dumps had been given to the right user; the folder they sat in had not. Roughly 50 GB accumulated, and by the time that experiment ran, every measurement was really measuring "writing a gigabyte to a nearly full disk". The note in the record is blunt: discard.

The second. A check meant to confirm the memory images were not corrupt reported all five as corrupt. It worked by looking for non-zero bytes near the middle and end of the image. The virtual machine was idle, so the top of its physical memory was mostly zeroes. The files were exactly the right size and perfectly valid. The probe was wrong, not the data.

How to test

The discipline adopted for this whole period was one rule: "no JSON, no claim." Nothing counted as a finding unless a machine-written results file backed it. That is what made both of these catchable — the contradiction showed up as a field reading zero, not as a vague sense that something was off.

Be honest about the limits

Two limits were recorded at the time and still hold. Every number here is a property of one host machine — a different server would re-open all of it. And the whole calibration was done against an idle guest, so it characterises the instrument, not the instrument under load.

This is the first appearance of a pattern that recurs three more times in this diary: a tool that gives a confident, wrong verdict about data that is fine. Entry 10 has the version of it that nearly invalidated a whole campaign.

Glossary

Hover any dotted term for its definition.

Snapshot
One complete copy of the virtual machine's memory, taken while the machine is frozen. The whole method is built on comparing consecutive ones.
Sampling interval
How long the guest is allowed to run between two snapshots. The knob this entry is about — and the one that turned out not to be connected to anything.
Pause fraction
The share of real time the guest spends frozen rather than running. Sampling faster does not cost proportionally more — it costs a steadily larger share of the machine's life.

The failure that wasn't

A seven-hour experiment failed all eighteen of its comparisons. Filed as a null result, it would have ended there. Asking why instead turned it into the largest single improvement in the project — about ninefold.

The one idea to hold onto

The experiment failed because the baseline already had the cure. It was comparing two fixed things and correctly finding no difference between them.

1
The setup

Eighteen failed comparisons

Entry 7 ended at a wall: the rhythm was detectable but under-sampled, and no analysis setting could fix it. The only route was to capture more snapshots per run. The suspected bottleneck was the memory copy, measured in the earlier campaign at a median of 7.06 seconds and eating 81% of every cycle.

So: seventy-two cells, four configurations — memory copy to a RAM disk or to the SSD, with cleanup on or off — and a gate requiring the fastest option to at least halve the baseline. A server reboot killed the run at cell 67; the last six were resumed and aggregated offline.

The gate failed on all eighteen comparisons. The RAM disk was only 1.35× faster, nowhere near the required 2×. And the baseline's memory copy did not measure 7 seconds. It measured 0.79.

The decision that mattered

The available move was to run another seven-hour experiment with a different lever. Instead the investigation went offline, against the original data — the timing records from the earlier campaign were still on disk, and nothing new needed capturing to ask why the baseline was nine times faster than the number it was supposed to reproduce.

2
The detective work

The seven-second number that was never a constant

The 7.06 seconds was a median — of a distribution running from 0.76 to 21.57 seconds. The pilot's 0.79 was not a contradiction; it was sitting on that distribution's floor. Which raises the real question: why did the old campaign's copies get slower as they went?

Because that campaign never deleted its memory images. The setting that enables delete-as-you-go was off in 125 of its 128 cells. Two independent fingerprints confirm it:

And the reason the gate could never have passed: the pilot enabled the streaming measurement — which is what makes deletion possible — on every arm, including the supposedly unmodified baseline. The experiment was comparing two already-fixed configurations.

Takeaway

A gate that fails on every comparison is evidence about the gate. Here the treatment had been accidentally applied to the control group, so the honest measured difference between them was, correctly, nothing.

The real result

What deletion actually bought

A controlled sixty-second head-to-head isolated it further: keeping everything piled up 21 GB in one minute and climbing, with copy times spiking; deleting held flat at 2.2 GB. Extrapolated to a ten-minute cell, the keep configuration is a 210 GB disk bomb.

Three previously published conclusions were retracted in one document — that the pilot was a dead end, that guest memory size was the real lever, and that the RAM disk was the win. The RAM disk is a 1.35× bonus on top of a ninefold effect that has nothing to do with it.

The retraction that got un-retracted

Entry 8's audit had struck out a storage claim as never exercised. This work exercises it — and passes the very disk ceiling that had been withdrawn as unmeasured. But it also dissolves the 7.06 s figure that the same audit had corrected the papers to. A claim can be wrong, correctly retracted, and then vindicated by a better experiment.

Be honest about the limits

The papers were frozen on 1 June and never learned any of this. They still print the 7.06 s median with no retention caveat, and still describe the disk ceiling as the biggest open item. Two later papers, written twelve hours after the first, do carry the caveat. The portfolio disagrees with itself.

One more failure worth recording. An early smoke test left 41 images and 44 GB behind in two minutes: the deletion call was failing silently because the folder belonged to the virtualisation service rather than the user. Deletion only helps if the delete succeeds — the same class of permission bug as Entry 5, in a different directory.

4
The measurement that was measuring nothing

"Duration" was a cap, not a target

With throughput fixed, the signal from two workloads was still nearly flat. They were doing their jobs correctly — and then stopping.

A workload told to run for 600 seconds finished its fixed amount of work — six million insertions, or a scan of five thousand files — in one to two seconds, and the machine idled for the remaining ten minutes. The duration setting was a ceiling, never a goal. So the cell recorded a couple of seconds of workload and ten minutes of an empty machine, and the average came out looking like nothing was happening.

The fix wraps the workload in a loop until the clock runs out. Measured directly afterwards: activity of 0.0875 sustained, against 0.0018 in the original — close to fifty times more signal from the same workload doing the same thing.

How to test

The tell is that the average is low while the peak is normal. A workload that is genuinely quiet is quiet throughout; a workload that finished early is briefly loud and then absent. Entry 10 finds this exact bug again, in a family that had never been checked for it.

With both fixed, the full sixty-six cell campaign ran clean: every cell succeeded, none starved of analysis windows — against 53 of 132 in the previous dataset — for 29,003 snapshot pairs and 7,159 windows. Repeat runs of the same workload agreed to the fourth decimal place.

Three things learned by accident

A workload writing 64 bytes per page registers almost the same activity as one rewriting the page entirely — the measurement counts pages touched, not bytes changed. One validation log contained 2.17 million copies of a single error line, which revealed that a workload had been silently hitting a default memory cap and streaming to disk instead. And the cells with the fewest snapshots were all continuous writers, because their disk traffic competes with the memory copy for the same device.

5
The blind spot

The twins, and the channel that split them

With a properly powered dataset, the honest classification numbers came in: telling threat-like from benign, on features that carry no label leakage, holding out an entire unseen workload — 0.636 against a 0.545 baseline. Real, but slight. Adding shape features (peak, variability, duty cycle) lifted it to 0.788 and lifted novelty detection from 0.739 to 0.925.

Then the harder test: hold out the entire threat family, so nothing similar has ever been seen. Three of the four encryptors scored zero. Their high memory activity makes them look exactly like the benign memory-intensive workloads once no example of their own kind is available to train on.

What survived that test
  • Novelty detection trained only on benign behaviour: 0.933 even with the whole threat family unseen.
  • A rule with no machine learning at all — count how many features fall outside the benign range — scores 0.95, matching the model and fully interpretable.
What did not

Supervised classification of an unseen family. And one pair stayed at zero in both directions: a slow trickling encryptor and a benign page-fault workload. Near-idle, rare spikes, opposite labels, mutually indistinguishable.

A prediction that was backwards, recorded as such

The expectation was that shape features would rescue the slow trickling encryptor. They rescued the bursty one instead (0.17 → 1.00) and a metadata scanner (0.50 → 1.00), and left the trickler at 0.00. Written into the results file as an explicit honest correction rather than quietly adjusted.

That stubborn pair is the entire premise of the next step. They are twins in memory, but one of them writes files and the other only touches memory — so the second channel is already there, unread. Adding a per-snapshot read of the guest's disk counters, at the same cadence and costing nothing extra since the machine is already paused:

plan06_results.md · result 1

The trickling encryptor writes 0.20 MB/s. The benign page-fault workload writes 0.002 MB/s. A hundredfold separation on exactly the pair that memory alone could not split.

The blind spot was not a limit of the method. It was a limit of looking at one channel.

6
The reframe

The heaviest writer is benign

The obvious next move is to declare disk activity a threat signal. The full ranking says otherwise, and this is the most thesis-shaping negative result in the project:

The heaviest writer in the corpus is benign — a read-modify-write traversal of a memory-mapped file, at 140 MB/s and reaching 231 MB/s over the full dataset. And a threat writes essentially nothing, because it holds its work in memory until a cap forces it out.

Takeaway

"Writes a lot to disk" is a behaviour, not a threat sign — exactly as memory activity turned out to be. Adding a channel does not add a detector. It adds a second axis on which behaviours that looked identical can be separated.

The full result confirms it precisely. Across the sixty-six cell dataset, adding the disk channel helps characterisation and hurts detection:

Family identity on a never-seen workload crosses the chance line that memory alone could not. Adding disk reads as well pushes workload identification to 0.985 — the two encrypt-in-place threats have a near-unique signature, reading plaintext and writing ciphertext — while doing nothing at all for detection, because three of the five threats barely read.

the sentence that reorganised the thesis

Two orthogonal channels disambiguate behaviours that one channel aliases. Not: the second channel catches the threat.

Characterisation, not detection. This is the input to everything in the final entry — including the decision to build a hundred workloads whose job is to be confused with each other in predictable ways.

Be honest about the limits

Stated at the time: the sixteen-cell subset is ten threats to six benign, so the majority-guess baseline is 0.625 and every cross-validated number from it falls below that. The trustworthy result there is the direct pair measurement — the hundredfold gap — not the classifier scores.

Glossary

Hover any dotted term for its definition.

Active page fraction
The share of memory pages that changed between two snapshots — one number per snapshot. The single scalar the later work asks whether it can live on.
Novelty detection
Training only on normal behaviour and flagging anything unlike it. Survives an unseen threat family, where a classifier trained on known classes scores zero by construction.
Held-out family
Removing an entire family of workloads from training. Harsher than holding out one workload, and the test that exposed how much of the earlier score was same-family memorisation.

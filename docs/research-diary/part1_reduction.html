The reduction

Before any detector, any dataset, any result — a chain of decisions about how to turn two gigabytes of memory into one number worth trusting. This entry is the reasoning. The verdict on whether it worked comes next.

The one idea to hold onto

Every step in this pipeline is a reduction, and each one was chosen for a physical reason before it was chosen for a mathematical one.

1
The founding move

Stop reading memory. Start listening to it.

Traditional memory forensics reads memory: parse the structures, walk the process list, identify what a region of bytes is for. That requires knowing what you're looking at — a different parser for every operating system, every version, every kind of process.

The move underneath everything here is to refuse that entirely. Never ask what a byte means. Only ask whether it changed. The abstract names the result a "MEMORY ECG," and the analogy is doing real work rather than decorating it — a cardiologist does not need to know which heart cells fired to read an irregular trace as irregular. That is the whole warrant for detecting something without knowing what it is.1

But the deeper framing underneath the ECG is musical, and it is the one that actually decides which tools get picked up later:

defence presentation, slide 08 · "Multi-Lens Analysis"

"Each routine is a 'note' in a symphony, played by a unique instrument. Memory operates like a complex musical arrangement. Anomalies alter the expected harmonics, disrupting stability."

If memory is a symphony, the natural question is about harmonic structure — which is why a pitch-detection technique borrowed from speech processing ends up in a memory-forensics project at all. Nobody reaches for that tool unless they already believe the target is something you listen to.

Analogy

Two doctors examine the same patient. One reads a chart of symptoms and looks each one up. The other puts a stethoscope to the chest and listens for whether the rhythm sounds right. Neither needs to know what disease they're hearing yet — only whether the rhythm has changed from what a healthy one sounds like.

A tell worth noticing

The symphony metaphor, and a companion one describing the data as a "memory blanket" stretched across pages and time, appear only in the slides used to persuade a room. Neither survives into the formal thesis text.2 The intuition is load-bearing and gets suppressed the moment the writing turns formal — a pattern worth watching for, because it means the plainest statement of the idea is not in the document that is supposed to contain it.

2
The reduction, named

Five reductions, one after another

"Listen, don't read" is the philosophy. What actually has to happen, mechanically, is a chain of five reductions — each one throwing away information on purpose, each one justified by what the next step needs and nothing more.

The move from "keep every snapshot" to "keep only what changed between them" is not just cheaper. It has a name in signal processing, and the theoretical notes reach for it directly. Fourier analysis has an exact, clean relationship between a function and its derivative:

differentiation property ℱ{ f′(t) }(ω) = jω · ℱ{ f(t) }(ω)

and the note beside it: this is "foundational in signal processing, especially when analysing how small changes (deltas) in the signal relate to the original signal."3 The claim underneath reduction 1 is not "a difference is a cheap trick." It is a difference is a derivative — and there is an entire discipline already built for what to do with one of those.

In plain terms

You cannot compare two-gigabyte files to each other directly and learn anything. So you cut the problem down, again and again, until what's left is small enough to reason about — and the whole question of whether the method works is really the question of whether each cut kept the right thing and threw away the wrong thing.

Takeaway

A method built this way can fail at any one of five separate steps without the others being wrong. That is not a weakness of the design — it is what makes each step checkable in isolation, which is exactly the habit Part Two spends a year building tools for.

3
Reduction 2 and 3, in detail

Two questions in every change

Reduction 2 asks one page two separate questions. How much changed — a count of individual bits that flipped, called the Hamming distance. And in what way — treat the page as a long list of numbers, and ask how far its direction moved, called the cosine distance.

Reduction 3 refuses to keep those as two separate numbers. It fuses them into one complex number — a length and an angle, together.

The justification given at the time is geometric: a complex number is the canonical container for exactly two things, a size and a direction, and the thesis cites Brown and Churchill's Complex Variables and Applications directly on this point.1 The reason that actually mattered downstream is more practical, and the defence deck says it more plainly than the thesis does — a genuinely complex-valued signal "enables frequency-based and phase-coherent analysis."2 Fourier transforms, cepstrum, complex wavelets — all of them natively expect a complex number. Feed them a single real value and half the machinery goes unused.

Where this earns its keep

One page, one value, and that value already knows both how much changed and in what direction. Every transform downstream inherits both facts for free, instead of having to track two separate numbers through the rest of the pipeline.

4
A second, quieter reduction

Where it happened is noise. What kind is signal.

A page's address in memory is close to arbitrary — the same program can allocate the same structure at a different address on every run, depending on the allocator's mood that day. So a representation that keys on where a change happened is measuring the memory allocator, not the program.

This is the reasoning behind reaching for wavelet scattering as a fourth lens later on, and the theoretical notes say the quiet part directly: the transform is chosen because it gives translation invariance — features "robust to changes in position (i.e., where the change occurs within the memory)."3

In plain terms

Two runs of the identical program, on the identical input, can dirty completely different addresses. If the method cares about address, it will call those two runs different. If it cares only about the shape of the change, it will correctly call them the same.

A thread that resurfaces

This exact reasoning — location is noise, pattern is signal — gets rediscovered from scratch a year later, applied to a completely different question. It is not a coincidence. It is the same intuition, load-bearing enough to survive a full reset of the project.

5
A recurring habit

Bracket everything, trust neither extreme

Two structural choices had to be made early and cannot be tuned away later: how big is one unit of comparison (a page, fixed at 4096 bytes), and how many pages get processed together at once (a chunk, fixed at 256 kilobytes). Neither number comes from a formula. Both come from the same reasoning move, applied twice.

A page too small "would introduce excessive noise due to minor fluctuations." A page too large "would capture fewer details, potentially overlooking significant but subtle changes."1 The 256-kilobyte chunk gets the identical treatment — too small and thread-management overhead dominates the work; too large and the parallelism that made per-page comparison affordable disappears.

Takeaway

Neither value is derived from theory. Both are placed in the middle of two named failure modes. That is a real form of justification, just not a formula — and it is worth naming as its own move, because it repeats at nearly every hyperparameter choice in this project, two years apart.

6
A principle, not yet a result

Never commit to one lens

Reduction 4 needs a transform — a way of turning a tiled block of these complex values into shape. There were four real candidates, each seeing something different in the same data.

Fourier gives the global rhythm, at the cost of assuming the rhythm doesn't change character over time. A wavelet transform gives a zoom lens instead — coarse structure and fine detail at once, described in the deck as "an adjustable zoom lens."2 Cepstrum asks whether the harmonic structure itself repeats. A fourth lens, wavelet scattering, gets added specifically because it needs no training data — fixed mathematical filters standing in for a neural network, chosen because labelled data was scarce.1

The habit, stated plainly

When it isn't clear in advance which representation is right, instrument all of them and let the comparison be the contribution. Not one model — a results table. This is why the thesis's central deliverable reads like a comparison rather than a verdict, and it is a habit that reappears at nearly every scale of this project afterward: three metrics per page, seven wavelet families, sixty-four feature columns two years later.

Be honest about the limits

Which lens actually wins is not decided here, on purpose. This entry is the reasoning before the measurement. What each lens actually scored — and the one result that made its own author refuse to believe it — is next.

Glossary

Hover any dotted term for its definition.

Cepstrum
A transform that looks for repeating patterns in a signal's frequency content rather than in the signal itself. Borrowed from speech processing, where it detects pitch.
Hamming distance
The count of individual bits that differ between two blocks of the same size. Measures how much of a page was rewritten between two snapshots — magnitude, not direction.
Cosine distance
Treat a page as a long list of numbers and ask how far its direction has rotated from where it was. Zero means the direction didn't change at all; it says nothing about how much the content changed.
Complex number encoding
Fusing the two page measurements — how much and which way — into one value with a length and an angle, instead of tracking them as two separate numbers through the rest of the pipeline.
Wavelet transform
A way of decomposing a signal that keeps both coarse, large-scale structure and fine, local detail at once — unlike a plain frequency transform, which sees the whole rhythm but loses exactly when each part happened.
Wavelet scattering transform
A cascade of wavelet transforms with fixed, untrained filters, producing features that don't shift just because the underlying pattern moved to a different location. Used here specifically because it needs no training data.
Translation invariance
A property where a representation gives the same answer regardless of where in the data the pattern occurred. The reasoning behind it: a memory address is close to arbitrary, so location shouldn't be allowed to matter.

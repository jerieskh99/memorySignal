What this file is, in five minutes

The aim. This is the plan for the next experiment — actually running the Berkeley dwarfs through your memory-capture — together with four experts' prediction of what that run will find. It is not results. It answers one thing: before we spend server time, what should we capture, what can we realistically learn, and how will one cheap test tell us whether the big campaign is worth it?

The whole story in a breath

The snapshot fires every 500 ms. A fast compute kernel sweeps its whole array thousands of times in that half-second, and the snapshot blends every sweep into one “everything got touched” picture. So the 13 dwarfs you wanted to tell apart blur down to only about 6–8 distinguishable behaviors. The guaranteed result is a map of which dwarfs are twins; the only open question is whether a few can be un-blurred; and one afternoon of testing decides it.

Your three questions, answered

1. Is he saying we get another deliverable — the confusion matrix?

Yes. Two outcomes, and both hand you a real result:

• If the dwarfs separate cleanly → you get a working classifier. The win.

• If they blur together → the confusion matrix is the deliverable. It is a table: “I ran dwarf X; the memory-signal called it Y.” It shows exactly which dwarfs memory-writes can and cannot tell apart. That is a real, publishable finding — not a failure. It is the floor you cannot fall below: you never walk away empty-handed.

2. Is that what we see if we run the dwarf?

Yes, exactly. Run a dwarf, look at its write-signal, and the matrix shows what it looks identical to. It is the empirical picture of “what does each dwarf's memory footprint actually look like, and which ones are indistinguishable twins.” It turns the whole question from a guess into a measured chart.

3. Are they saying 500 ms is too big an interval?

Yes — that is the central physical finding, for the compute kernels. In 500 ms a fast kernel sweeps its array thousands of times; the snapshot ORs all those sweeps into one flat frame, so the internal motion disappears. The camera blinks too slowly to catch the moving parts.

But here is the twist that matters: the fix is not “shrink the interval.” 500 ms is the fixed production cadence, and a finer one means far more overhead and data. So instead of speeding up the camera, they slow down the subject — make the kernel bigger so a single sweep takes longer than 500 ms.

Like photographing a spinning fan: you can't make the shutter faster, so you slow the fan until a single blade holds still long enough to see.

That trick only works where the compute grows faster than the memory it uses — QR and LU qualify (grow them ~2×–4× and one sweep finally spans several snapshots). Stencils and their kind never can: to slow them you'd need tens of gigabytes, far past the 1 GiB guest. Those stay a permanent blur — you will only ever see their size, never their motion.

The plan itself — one cheap afternoon, gated

  1. Gate 0 — pure arithmetic (done): which kernels can be grown enough to un-blur? QR and LU yes; stencil, DP, FFT no.
  2. Gate 1 — seconds on the server, no capture: run enlarged QR and LU, check they really slow down as predicted.
  3. Gate 2 — the one decisive check: capture gemm for ~15 s and see whether an array's changed pages sit together or scatter. Every “shape” feature depends on this.
  4. Gate 3 — the pilot proper: capture the enlarged QR/LU (do they show motion?), a few controls, and one deliberately-un-rescuable stencil as a trap that should stay blurred.

GO — enlarged QR/LU show their motion → the campaign is worth it, run the full set.
NO-GO — they stay blurred → motion is unrecoverable, and Part 2's honest result is the confusion matrix plus a size-and-magnitude classifier. Ship that, stop there.

Either way you have a thesis result. The pilot only decides whether it is the ambitious version (you can read a dwarf's motion) or the honest modest one (you can read its shape and name its twins).
